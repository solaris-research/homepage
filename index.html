Solaris Research

Frontier safety science · Zürich, Switzerland

Solaris Research is a technical AI safety research non-profit by researchers at ETH Zürich. We build the science to detect, understand and prevent failures in frontier models: independent, open safety science the world can rely on.

A safety claim is a statement about an AI system across a space of conditions: architecture, scale, inference budget, tools and deployment harness. While most harms from AI arise from large closed models released as agents at massive scale1, most scientific evidence is collected at one point in that space: on small dense open models in single chat conversations. As such, we have almost no understanding of how a model’s risk profile shifts after release: under narrow fine-tuning, in long-context multi-turn interactions, inside a harness, or generally in RSI conditions2.

Resources to mitigate the thousands of documented AI harms3 are finite and scientists need to do what they can to support policymakers in deciding which threats to prioritise. Our ICML 2026 oral position paper (top 0.7%), Anthropomorphic Misalignment Research Needs Stronger Evidence, provides a calibration to grade misalignment claims by strength of evidence. It is a first step towards building a science of misalignment where anthropomorphic claims, for example on deception and scheming, are weighed by causal support, not by alarm.

From research to governance. The stakes are rising fast: protection from AI harms is not only a technical problem for scientists to solve, nor a Western preference, but a universal right and should be treated as such. Securing it calls for open and rigorous technical research that policymakers can depend on: the kind of independent work Solaris Research is on a mission to do.

We work on

Our research bet on a science of misalignment organises four connected directions:

  • Open verification. Verifying safety claims independently, we build tools that evaluate misalignment and share our work openly, as with Apertus Claritas, the public hub for safety research on open-weight models.
  • Training dynamics. Tracing how safety behaviour forms from pre-training to fine-tuning, we study preference instability in reward models, safe reward-model optimisation and how RLHF can mislead humans.
  • Agentic attack surfaces. Testing whether models remain under control once they act as agents, we study how emergently misaligned LLMs cause agentic misconduct, benchmark LLMs on cryptographic schemes and automate prompt injection with RL.
  • Mechanistic intervention. Locating where safety representations emerge within a model, we show that local sparsity detects unsafe outputs without supervision and that learned geometric polytopes or probe-based calibrated steering can reverse unsafe behaviour.
